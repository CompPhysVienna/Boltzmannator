Boltzmann generators & normalising flows

This app shows, in one dimension, the idea behind Boltzmann generators: learn an invertible transformation that turns a simple, easy-to-sample distribution into the Boltzmann distribution of a physical system.

1 · The sampling problem

In equilibrium statistical mechanics a configuration x occurs with probability

p*(x) = e−U(x)/kBT / Z ,

where U(x) is the potential energy, kBT the temperature and Z the (usually unknown) partition function. When U has several deep wells, p* concentrates on well-separated regions, and molecular dynamics or Monte Carlo need very long runs to hop between them. A Boltzmann generator sidesteps the problem: it produces independent samples in one shot, by transforming samples of a simple distribution.

2 · Change of variables

Start from a latent variable z with a simple density   pz(z) (top-left panel) and apply an invertible, differentiable map   x = fθ(z) (centre panel). The transformed variable has the density

px(x) = pz(z) |dfθ/dz|−1 ,  z = fθ−1(x) .

Where the map stretches (Jacobian |J| = |dfθ/dz| > 1) probability is spread out and the density drops; where it compresses, the density rises. The bottom-left panel shows exactly this factor |J|−1. Because fθ is invertible, px is automatically normalised — this construction is called a normalising flow.

3 · Training the flow

The parameters θ are optimised by gradient descent so that px matches the target   p*. The app offers the two standard objectives:

Energy-based (reverse Kullback–Leibler divergence). Minimise

L = ⟨ U(fθ(z))/kBT − log |J(z)| ⟩z∼pz = KL(px ‖ p*) + const .

Only the energy function is needed — no samples of the target. The first term pulls the samples into the low-energy wells; the second (an entropy) keeps the distribution from collapsing onto the minimum.

Example-based (forward KL, maximum likelihood). Given data points xi ∼ p*, minimise the negative log-likelihood

NLL = ⟨ −log pz( fθ−1(x)) + log |J| ⟩data = KL(p* ‖ px) + const .

The loss panel shows both contributions separately (blue and orange) together with their sum (dark line).

4 · Reweighting

A trained flow is close to, but not exactly, the target. Averages can nevertheless be computed exactly by importance sampling: each sample x is weighted with

w(x) = p*(x) / px(x) ,  ⟨A⟩ = Σ wiA(xi) / Σ wi .

The effective sample size Neff = (Σw)2/Σw2 measures the overlap between px and p* (tick Show importance weights after sampling).

5 · The transformation families

Polynomial maps are simple but not guaranteed to be invertible — the app marks turning points and falls back to a histogram estimate of px. The single-layer perceptron adds K sigmoid steps with positive weights to a rising line, so it is strictly monotone, hence invertible. The rational-quadratic spline is a flexible monotone spline on [−B, B], the building block of modern neural spline flows.

In one dimension an exact transformation always exists, x*(z) = F*−1(Fz(z)), built from the two cumulative distributions (tick Show exact transformation). The real power of flows shows in many dimensions, where no such formula exists and the map must be learned.

What you can do here

Shape a simple latent density into a target Boltzmann distribution — by hand with the sliders, or by training. Hover over any control for a short explanation of what it does.

The figure

  • Latent density pz(z) — top left; choose and shape it on the Densities tab.
  • Transformation x = fθ(z) — centre; orange dots appear where it is not invertible.
  • Jacobian |J|−1 — bottom left; the local stretch factor that reshapes the density.
  • Transformed density px(x) — drawn sideways next to the map, sharing its x-axis.
  • Target p*(x) — overlaid when Show target is ticked (automatically during training).
  • Right column: histograms of the latent and transformed samples, and the training loss.

Densities tab

Pick the latent distribution and set its mean and width. Define the target p*(x) ∝ e−U(x)/kT through the coefficients of the potential U(x) = u₁x + u₂x² + u₃x³ + u₄x⁴ and the temperature kT. Show exact transformation draws the map that would reproduce the target perfectly.

Map tab

Choose the transformation family and move its parameters — every slider updates the figure live. Show mapping lines visualises how individual points z travel to x = fθ(z). Defaults restores the identity map, Randomize throws the dice.

Training tab

Sample! draws N latent points and pushes them through the map; Data generates example points from the target. Choose the objective (energy-based needs only the potential, example-based needs the data), an optimiser, the number of epochs, learning rate and batch size, then press Train!. Every/Delay control how often the figure updates — increase the delay to watch the flow deform in slow motion. Stop interrupts; Reset training clears the loss history and restores the identity map.

Things to try

  1. Double well. On Densities, set u₂ = −2 and u₄ ≈ 0.3, tick Show target, then Train! — watch one Gaussian split into two peaks.
  2. Slow motion. Set Delay to 200 ms and Every to 1 to see every single epoch.
  3. Break invertibility. Choose the polynomial map and push θ₂ or θ₃ until the orange dots appear — the density then needs a histogram estimate (KDE).
  4. Bimodal latent. Match a bimodal latent to a double-well target — a much easier job for the map.
  5. Compare optimisers. Train the same problem with SGD and with Adam at different learning rates.
  6. Learn from data. Press Data, switch to Example-based, and train from samples instead of energies.
  7. Check the quality. After training, press Sample! and tick Show importance weights: Neff near 100% means px ≈ p*.

General

Auto-rescale recomputes the axes after every change — untick it to freeze them for comparisons. The switch at the bottom toggles dark mode, and the dots next to the portrait change the picture. Reset restores the whole app to its initial state.
